# God's Great Story

## Thirteen weeks to understand the gospel · Ages 8–12

A Sunday school curriculum from River of Grace Church. Thirteen weekly meetings cover a quarter; if a class is missed, continue the sequence. No pictures or internet connection are required after printing.

**Purpose:** help children explain in their own words who God is, why we need redemption, who Jesus is, what he has done, and how we respond to the gospel. Repeating an answer is not the same as understanding it and does not demonstrate conversion.

**Suggested length:** 50–60 minutes. Welcome and review, 5; opening case, 7; Bible reading and explanation, 15; discussion, 10; activity and worksheet, 15; closing prayer, 5. Shorten the activity for a 45-minute class.

**Materials:** a Bible, paper, pencils, and a board. Read passages in the translation used by your church. Explanations here are paraphrases, not quotations from a particular translation. Check the text before printing literal Bible quotations. The memory reference lets you choose the verse in that same translation.

**Teaching approach:** ask questions and listen before correcting. Ask “Why?” and return to the passage. For ages 8–9, read together and allow drawings or spoken answers; for ages 10–12, ask for a reason and a possible objection. Do not request public confessions or personal details. The cases are fictional. Children may choose not to share. Do not use threats, emotional pressure, or a repeated prayer as evidence of salvation. If a child asks about faith, listen carefully and involve the family according to the church's safeguarding procedures.

**Three distinctions:** God's justice does not depend on what benefits me; his goodness does not mean granting every wish; love is not merely an emotion, although it can include genuine affection. God is neither indifferent nor cold. His holiness, justice, and love are consistent: the Father sends the Son in love, and the Son gives himself willingly.

## 1 · God: holy, just, and good

**Goal:** distinguish God's character from our preferences. **Read:** Deuteronomy 32:4; Psalm 119:68; Isaiah 6:1–5. **Memory:** Deuteronomy 32:4.

**Opening case.** A referee changes a rule so her daughter's team wins. The daughter says, “My mom is so good!” The other team objects. Ask: “Does helping me win make a decision good? Who is thinking only about what benefits them?”

**Teaching explanation.** God is the Creator; we did not invent him and do not decide what he should be like. Holy means that God is incomparable, pure, and completely separate from evil. It does not simply mean he is stronger or lives far away. Just means that he always acts rightly: he judges truthfully, without favoritism, and never calls evil good. He knows even what no witness saw. Good means that his character is perfect and he does what is right and beneficial according to his wisdom. He is the source and standard of goodness: he does not arbitrarily make cruelty or lying good. He never acts against his own character.

A child may call someone “good” for giving them everything they want. But allowing that child to hurt someone else would not be goodness. A refusal or just correction can seek their good. God does not promise a comfortable life or explain every sorrow now. We must not conclude that a particular illness or tragedy punishes something the child did. We can bring God our questions and sadness without pretending to understand everything.

**Discuss.** Was the referee just even though her daughter was happy? Can a decision upset me and still be good? What does the passage say about God's works? If I do not understand something, does that prove God is unjust? Why must we know his character to trust him?

**Activity.** Make three columns: “I like it,” “It is just,” and “It seeks someone's good.” Consider sharing exam answers, returning money received by mistake, stopping someone from crossing a dangerous street, and correcting a friend without humiliating them. An action can fit several columns. Ask for reasons; do not vote to determine truth.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Explain “just” without using “I like it.” 2. Why would granting all my wishes not always be good? 3. Complete: “God is holy, so…”. 4. Write an honest question about God. **Expected answer:** right judgment without favoritism; some wishes harm people; God is pure and does not approve of evil. Do not grade the personal question.

**At home.** Discuss a family rule that protects others. Parents can acknowledge their mistakes; no human authority is perfect like God. **Closing:** thank God for his goodness and ask for help to understand it even when we do not like everything.

## 2 · Made in his image

**Goal:** explain why all people have dignity and responsibility before God. **Read:** Genesis 1:26–31; James 3:9–10. **Memory:** Genesis 1:27.

**Opening case.** Two students choose teams. They leave out the slowest runner, saying, “He is useless.” Have they measured his running ability or his worth as a person?

**Teaching explanation.** God created human beings in his image: to represent him in his creation, know him, and live responsibly before him. This does not mean God has our bodies. Men and women receive this dignity. Intelligence, money, strength, appearance, and abilities do not make anyone more God's image than another. Nor does human dignity disappear when a person sins.

We were created to love God and our neighbors, care for what he entrusts to us, and live in fellowship with him. Being creatures means depending on God; being his image means our lives have value and purpose. Dignity does not remove responsibility: mistreating someone dishonors the God whose image they bear. James connects our understanding of creation with how we speak.

**Discuss.** Why does the classmate matter even if he cannot run well? What changes when we remember that an unpleasant person also bears God's image? What responsibility do we have toward animals and nature? Why is dignity not permission to do anything we want?

**Activity.** Give out role cards: new student, older person, child with a disability, athlete, and someone who has done wrong. For each, write one way to show respect and one responsibility. Do not imitate disabilities or turn them into a game.

**Student worksheet.** 1. “A person matters because…”. 2. Correct: “Only well-behaved people deserve respect.” 3. Suggest a way to include the classmate in the case. **Expected answer:** God created people in his image; we can correct behavior without despising a person; include them respectfully with reasonable adjustments.

**At home.** Identify an expression that demeans someone and replace it with truthful, respectful words. **Closing:** thank God for people and ask for help to treat them with dignity.

## 3 · Sin and the heart

**Goal:** recognize that sin includes desires and decisions against God as well as visible actions. **Read:** Genesis 3:1–13; Mark 7:20–23. **Memory:** Romans 3:23.

**Opening case.** Andrew tears up his sister's project so she will not receive praise. He then calls it an accident. Did the problem begin when he tore the paper or earlier?

**Teaching explanation.** In Genesis 3, the serpent questions God's word and goodness. Human beings disobey and want to decide good and evil for themselves. Then they hide and blame others. Sin is rebellion against God: doing, desiring, or failing to do things contrary to his will. Jesus teaches that evil also comes from the heart. Here “heart” includes thoughts, desires, and will, not just feelings.

We all need forgiveness. Comparing ourselves with someone worse does not make us innocent. Sin damages our relationship with God and others; it is not simply a technical mistake. However, feeling angry or sad is not automatically sin, and an unwanted thought is not the same as embracing it and acting on it. Being a victim of injustice does not make a child guilty of what someone else did to them. Our dignity remains, although we need God to rescue and transform us.

**Discuss.** What did Andrew want? Why did lying make the harm worse? How are his excuses like those in Genesis? Why does changing only outward behavior fail to solve the problem of the heart?

**Activity.** Draw a tree: roots = desires; trunk = decisions; fruit = actions and consequences. Fill it in using the fictional case. Then describe what Andrew would need to acknowledge the wrong without excuses.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Name two sins in the case. 2. Write an excuse and an honest confession Andrew might make. 3. Does being better than another person remove our need for forgiveness? **Expected answer:** envy, deliberate harm, lying; admitting what he did without blaming his sister; no.

**At home.** An adult can model a specific apology without demanding a confession from the child. **Closing:** acknowledge our need for mercy without requesting public personal accounts.

## 4 · The commandments: love and a mirror

**Goal:** connect God's law with his character and understand that our performance cannot save us. **Read:** Exodus 20:1–17; Matthew 22:34–40; Romans 3:19–20. **Memory:** Matthew 22:39.

**Opening case.** A girl looks in a mirror and sees mud on her face. She rubs the mirror to clean herself. What can the mirror show? What can it not do?

**Teaching explanation.** God rescued Israel from Egypt before giving the commandments at Sinai. The law reveals his will and directs a life that honors God and neighbor. His commandments are not pointless obstacles: the command against stealing protects our neighbor, and the command against lying expresses the importance of truth. Jesus summarizes the law as loving God and our neighbor. Love does not give us permission to ignore his commandments.

The law also reveals our sin; Romans says that through it we become aware of sin. Like a mirror, it exposes what pretending or comparing ourselves cannot fix. The comparison has limits: the law is God's living, authoritative word, not a passive object. It teaches and guides, but our obedience does not buy forgiveness. We need the Savior. Game rules and family preferences do not have the same authority as God's commandments.

**Discuss.** Whom does the command against stealing protect? Can someone avoid stealing outwardly while nurturing greed? Why does summarizing the law as love not make it optional? What is wrong with believing enough good deeds erase bad ones?

**Activity.** Connect three commandments with the good they protect. Then consider: “I return the stolen pencil so people will praise me.” Distinguish the right action from a motive that still needs examination.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Explain the mirror comparison and its limit. 2. Choose a commandment and explain how it relates to love. 3. Can we buy forgiveness by obeying from tomorrow onward? **Expected answer:** the law reveals sin but does more than a mirror; answers connected to God and neighbor; no.

**At home.** Read one commandment and ask whom it protects. **Closing:** ask God to reveal our sin and teach us to love.

## 5 · Justice and mercy

**Goal:** understand why ignoring evil is not justice and why guilty people need mercy. **Read:** Romans 2:1–4; 3:23–26. **Memory:** Romans 3:26.

**Opening case.** Someone destroys a classmate's bicycle. The adult in charge knows what happened but says, “I am so good that I will do nothing.” Has the adult cared for the classmate? Has the wrong been resolved?

**Teaching explanation.** When we suffer injustice, we want the truth told and the harm addressed. God is not indifferent to evil. But Romans prevents us from always placing ourselves in the judge's seat: we also sin. His judgment is true and shows no favoritism. Justice does not mean impulsive revenge or permission for children to punish others.

Mercy is compassion for someone in need; in relation to guilt, it includes receiving a good we cannot demand as though we were innocent. The gospel does not say God pretended sin never happened. Romans presents Christ as the answer that displays God's justice when he justifies those who have faith in Jesus. We will return to the cross in week 9. Human forgiveness does not remove every consequence or require a victim to return to danger. Seeking a responsible adult's help is compatible with forgiveness.

**Discuss.** What is wrong with the adult's decision? Why are we not innocent judges of everybody else? Are mercy and saying evil was acceptable the same thing? Why do we need an answer that takes both guilt and God's love seriously?

**Activity.** Compare responses: ignore it; humiliate the guilty person; establish what happened, protect the harmed child, and address the damage with a just consequence. Explain what each response recognizes. Repairing a bicycle does not by itself represent how salvation is received.

**Student worksheet.** 1. “Ignoring evil is not goodness because…”. 2. Distinguish justice from revenge. 3. Why do I need mercy? **Expected answer:** it abandons the harmed person and leaves wrong unaddressed; right judgment versus a desire to make someone suffer; I am also a sinner.

**At home.** Talk about seeking help when there is injustice, without demanding immediate reconciliation. **Closing:** ask for justice and mercy, including for ourselves.

## 6 · God promises blessing and rescue

**Goal:** connect Bible stories within God's plan fulfilled in Christ. **Read:** Genesis 12:1–3; 15:1–6; Galatians 3:7–9, 16. **Memory:** Genesis 12:3.

**Opening case.** Someone reads scattered pages from a book: a journey, a family, a king. They think these are unrelated stories. What are they missing?

**Teaching explanation.** After the fall, God does not abandon his creation. Genesis 3:15 announces conflict and victory over the serpent; the story gradually unfolds the promise of rescue. God calls Abraham and promises blessing reaching the families of the earth. Abraham is not presented as a sinless hero; he depends on God's promise. Genesis 15 emphasizes his faith.

The story continues with Israel, the exodus, the law, David's kingdom, and the prophets. These are not interchangeable episodes: each has its context and shows God's faithfulness, human sin, and the need for fulfillment. Galatians identifies Christ as the offspring in whom the promise is fulfilled. God works sovereignly and people make decisions for which they are responsible. We do not need to solve every question about how these relate today to recognize what the text affirms.

**Discuss.** Was the promised blessing for only one family? Why do Abraham's failures not destroy God's faithfulness? Who connects the promise with its fulfillment? How does following the story's thread change the way we read?

**Activity.** Arrange cards: creation, fall, Abraham, exodus, David, prophets, Jesus, church, new creation. Draw a connecting thread and write “God keeps his promises.” The church lives after Christ's coming and awaits his return.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Put four stages in order. 2. What did God promise Abraham concerning the families of the earth? 3. In whom is the promise fulfilled according to Galatians? **Expected answer:** the activity's order; blessing; Christ.

**At home.** Choose a familiar Bible story and place it on the timeline. **Closing:** thank God for his faithfulness.

## 7 · Rescue and the need for a better sacrifice

**Goal:** understand how Passover and sacrifices prepare us to understand Christ, without assigning them independent saving power. **Read:** Exodus 12:1–14; John 1:29; Hebrews 10:1–14. **Memory:** John 1:29.

**Opening case.** A sign announces a bridge ahead. A traveler hugs the sign and says they have crossed the river. What is the sign for, and why is it not the bridge?

**Teaching explanation.** Israel was enslaved in Egypt. At Passover, God gave specific instructions and rescued his people amid judgment. Read soberly, without dramatizing deaths or adding graphic details. Deliverance was God's work; the people were to trust his word and obey it.

Sacrifices taught the seriousness of sin and the need to approach God as he directed. Hebrews explains that animal blood could not take away sins definitively and that repeated sacrifices pointed to something greater. Jesus is presented as the Lamb of God. He is not an animal or one more repetition: he offers himself willingly, and his sacrifice is sufficient. The sign comparison helps explain pointing toward fulfillment; the biblical rites had a real function in Israel's life and were not worthless signs.

**Discuss.** Who rescued Israel? What did the repetition of sacrifices show according to Hebrews? Why do we not need animal sacrifices to approach God? What distinguishes Jesus from earlier sacrifices?

**Activity.** Make two columns from Hebrews: repeated sacrifices / Christ's offering once for all. Find the reasons for each difference in the passage.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Explain “pointing toward” using the sign case. 2. Could animal blood remove sin definitively? 3. Why is Jesus' offering sufficient? **Expected answer:** it announces something it does not replace; no; he fulfills God's will and offers his own life definitively.

**At home.** Read John 1:29 and recall two things learned about Jesus. **Closing:** thank God for the rescue he provides.

## 8 · Jesus: truly God and truly human

**Goal:** explain why Jesus can reveal God and be our Redeemer. **Read:** John 1:1–18; Mark 2:1–12; Hebrews 4:15. **Memory:** John 1:14.

**Opening case.** A spectator shouts instructions to a team but does not enter the game. Ask: “What is the difference between advising from outside and entering to help?” Explain that Jesus does far more than help in a game.

**Teaching explanation.** John teaches that the Word was with God and was God, and that he became flesh. Jesus did not begin to exist at birth: the eternal Son came as a true human being. He is not half God and half human, or a man promoted to God. He is one person, the Son, truly God and truly human. He experienced tiredness, hunger, and pain; he was tempted and did not sin. We can acknowledge depth here without using “mystery” to assert contradictions.

In Mark 2, Jesus forgives sins and heals the paralyzed man, showing his authority. He did not come merely as an example or teacher: he came to save. He is not kinder than the Father; he reveals him. The game comparison only illustrates distance and nearness; it does not explain his divine nature or how he saves. Avoid analogies such as water/ice/steam to explain God: they can confuse the Father, Son, and Spirit.

**Discuss.** What does John say about Jesus before his birth? What shows he was really human? Why was his forgiving sins surprising? What would be missing from a message that only said “imitate Jesus”?

**Activity.** Find evidence in the passages for “Jesus is truly human” and “Jesus has divine identity and authority.” These describe the same person; do not divide him into two characters.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Correct: “Jesus began to exist in Bethlehem.” 2. Does being tempted mean he sinned? 3. Give a reason we need more than a good example. **Expected answer:** the Son is eternal; no; we need forgiveness, reconciliation, and transformation.

**At home.** Read Mark 2 and ask what problem Jesus addressed besides paralysis. **Closing:** thank God that the Son came to rescue us.

## 9 · The cross and the resurrection

**Goal:** connect Jesus' death with our sins and his resurrection with real hope. **Read:** Isaiah 53:4–6; John 10:17–18; 1 Corinthians 15:1–8, 17–22. **Memory:** 1 Corinthians 15:3–4.

**Opening case.** A friend breaks something you own. You forgive them and pay for its repair. The damage did not disappear: someone bore a cost. What does this show about forgiveness? The comparison does not fully explain the cross.

**Teaching explanation.** Jesus lived without sin and died for our sins. At the cross he was not suffering for wrongs of his own: he gave himself for sinners. Isaiah announces the servant who bears our iniquities. His offering was not a fight between a cruel Father and a compassionate Son; the Father sends in love and the Son gives his life willingly. God takes sin seriously and offers reconciliation by grace.

Jesus really died, was buried, and rose bodily. Paul mentions witnesses and explains that without the resurrection our faith would be futile. Resurrection does not simply mean his friends kept his memory alive. Christ lives, has defeated death, and those who belong to him have the hope of resurrection. We do not need violent scenes to teach this. Repairing an object illustrates a cost, but sin is not merely a material bill, and no child should think they can replace Jesus by paying a debt.

**Discuss.** Why did Jesus die if he had not sinned? Did he give himself against his will? What would change if he had remained dead? What part of the gospel do we forget if we speak only about the cross?

**Activity.** Order four cards from 1 Corinthians 15: died for our sins; was buried; rose again; appeared to witnesses. Explain each without adding an invented fifth stage.

**Student worksheet.** 1. “Christ died for…”. 2. Explain why the resurrection matters. 3. Where does the repaired-object comparison fall short? **Expected answer:** our sins; Christ lives and there is victory over sin and death; salvation is not a material repair anyone can pay for.

**At home.** Let the child explain the four cards to an adult. **Closing:** give thanks to the living Savior.

## 10 · The love we know in Christ

**Goal:** define love by God's character and work, distinguishing it from liking, permissiveness, or exchange. **Read:** 1 John 4:7–11; John 15:9–13; 1 Corinthians 13:4–7. **Memory:** 1 John 4:10.

**Opening case.** A friend asks you to lie to cover up their hurting someone. They say, “If you loved me, you would.” Does loving them mean helping them continue to harm others?

**Teaching explanation.** We know love because God loved us and sent his Son. To love is to seek another person's true good according to God's truth, with a disposition expressed in actions, even at a cost. It is not merely feeling affection, but affection and compassion can belong to love. We do not teach that feelings are bad or that loving means acting coldly.

First Corinthians says love rejoices in truth, not wrongdoing. Love may therefore include refusing, correcting patiently, or asking for help. Christ's sacrifice shows holy love: it seeks our reconciliation with God, not merely immediate relief. Serving others does not make us their savior. No child must endure abuse, keep dangerous secrets, or expose themselves to harm to prove love. They may leave and seek help.

**Discuss.** Why does lying not seek the friend's good? Can someone feel affection and act selfishly? Can we do what is right when we do not feel like it? How does the cross show something greater than a passing emotion?

**Activity.** Classify with reasons: giving a present to get one back; sharing out of compassion; correcting without humiliation; covering up an assault; accompanying someone to seek help. Do not guess classmates' actual motives: examine only the written case.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Define love using “good” and “truth.” 2. Answer the friend without despising them. 3. Explain what God did that reveals his love. **Expected answer:** seeking true good; refusing to lie and offering to accompany the friend in telling the truth; God sent his Son for us.

**At home.** Choose a specific act of service without a reward or public display. **Closing:** ask for love that rejoices in truth.

## 11 · Grace, repentance, and faith

**Goal:** distinguish salvation received by grace from attempts to deserve it. **Read:** Ephesians 2:1–10; Mark 1:14–15; Luke 18:9–14. **Memory:** Ephesians 2:8–9.

**Opening case.** Someone receives a gift and wants to pay so they can say, “I earned it.” What changes if they turn it into wages? Can an empty hand receive something valuable?

**Teaching explanation.** Ephesians describes our need and God's merciful initiative: salvation is by grace through faith, not works, so nobody can boast. Grace is favor we do not deserve. Faith does not mean manufacturing an intense emotion or believing everything will happen as I wish. It is trusting Jesus Christ and his work because he is trustworthy.

Repentance means acknowledging sin and turning to God instead of defending it or merely trying to avoid consequences. Faith and repentance are not payments that put God under obligation; they are the response to the gospel that his grace makes possible. Good works appear in verse 10 as the fruit and purpose of received life, not its price. A prayer can express faith, but repeating words or raising a hand does not guarantee salvation. Invite questions and continued listening to God's word without manipulating a decision.

**Discuss.** How do the two men in Luke 18 differ? Why does a list of achievements not erase guilt? How is regretting being caught different from repenting? Where does Ephesians place good works?

**Activity.** Arrange three cards: God's grace → salvation received through faith → a life of good works. Examine why reversing the order changes the message. The arrow summarizes the passage; it does not measure the inner moment of a child's conversion.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Define grace and faith. 2. Correct: “If I do enough good works, God will owe me salvation.” 3. Write a question you still have about Jesus. **Expected answer:** undeserved favor and trust in Christ; salvation is a gift, not a debt; welcome questions without grading them.

**At home.** Read Ephesians 2:8–10 and distinguish gift from fruit. **Closing:** offer a simple prayer of thanks; nobody must repeat it to prove faith.

## 12 · A new life learning to love

**Goal:** understand that obedience grows from grace and produces specific changes without demanding instant perfection. **Read:** Luke 19:1–10; Galatians 5:22–23; 1 John 1:8–9. **Memory:** Ephesians 2:10.

**Opening case.** A girl says she asked forgiveness for taking a notebook, but keeps it. Another thinks returning it will force God to forgive her. What does each misunderstand?

**Teaching explanation.** Jesus seeks and saves the lost. In his meeting with Zacchaeus, we see a concrete response concerning money and harm done. Restitution does not buy salvation, but repentance does not treat harm as unimportant. When possible and appropriate, acknowledging wrong leads to returning, repairing, and changing our conduct.

Christian life is not simply trying hard on our own. The Spirit produces fruit: love, joy, peace, patience, and the other qualities in the passage. We learn through the word, prayer, and the church community. We still need to confess our sins. A failure does not mean Jesus stopped being sufficient; neither does it excuse continuing without repentance. Confidence rests in Christ and his promises, not comparing our progress with others or remembering a religious gesture.

**Discuss.** Why does returning the notebook matter? Why does it not buy forgiveness? What help does someone who wants to change need? How should they respond if they fail again? How does patience differ from pretending nothing happened?

**Activity.** Address the case in four steps: acknowledge without excuses; ask forgiveness; repair what is possible with responsible help; plan different conduct. If returning something involves danger, seek adult guidance first.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Distinguish fruit from price. 2. Write a responsible response to the case. 3. Name two means by which God helps us grow. **Expected answer:** the result of received life versus merit to obtain it; acknowledgment and returning the notebook; the Spirit through the word, prayer, and community.

**At home.** Practice a task of service together without making it a spiritual score. **Closing:** ask the Spirit for help to grow in love.

## 13 · The whole story and our hope

**Goal:** tell the gospel story, connecting creation, fall, promise, Christ, and new creation. **Read:** Revelation 21:1–5; Ephesians 2:19–22; 1 Corinthians 15:20–22. **Memory:** Revelation 21:4.

**Opening case.** A story ends with a rescue, but nobody tells us what happened to the rescued people. What is missing? The Bible takes rescue toward life with God and a renewed creation.

**Teaching explanation.** God gathers a people belonging to Christ. The church is a family where we learn, worship, serve, and care for one another; it is not a group of superior people. Jesus rose and will return. Biblical hope is not escaping forever from all material creation: God promises new creation, life with him, and the end of sin, death, and sorrow.

While we wait, we love our neighbors and respectfully share the good news. We do not set dates or promise Christians will not suffer now. When a child is sad, listen; future hope does not require them to stop crying today. The story shows the faithfulness of the holy, just, and good God who fulfills his purpose through Christ.

**Discuss.** What does God promise to remove in Revelation? Why does Jesus' resurrection matter? How does the church help us today? How would you tell the gospel to someone who has never heard the story?

**Final activity.** Make a six-page book: God and his good creation; human beings in his image; sin and brokenness; promise and preparation; Jesus crucified and risen; responding in faith and living a new life while awaiting new creation. Give each page an original sentence and a Bible reference. Drawing is optional; explaining the connections is central.

**Student worksheet.** 1. Explain why we need Jesus. 2. What did he do? 3. How is salvation received? 4. What changes afterward? 5. What do we await? **Expected answer:** sin and the need for reconciliation; he died for our sins and rose; by grace through faith, with repentance; a life of love and obedience by the Spirit; resurrection and life with God in the new creation.

**At home.** Listen to the child tell their book without correcting every sentence as they speak. Then reread a passage together that helps with a question. **Closing:** thank God for his faithfulness and ask for help to live in his hope.

## Checking understanding: a guide for teachers

In weeks 1, 5, 9, and 13, briefly note which ideas the group needs explained again. Do not publish comparisons between children or grade their faith. At the end, listen for reasons in their answers: Why is God good even when he does not grant every wish? Why is sin more than bad behavior? Why do works not buy salvation? Why do we need the cross and resurrection? How are love and truth connected?

If a child says “God loves me because I behave,” return to Ephesians 2. If they say “The Father did not love us and Jesus persuaded him,” return to 1 John 4. If they say “Love means letting people do anything,” return to 1 Corinthians 13. If they say “I must not feel sad anymore,” listen and return to Revelation's hope without denying present pain.
